# Week 5 Lab Overview — Sensors, Noise, and Uncertainty

This individual, self-contained Streamlit lab examines how imperfect measurements affect robotic estimation and decision-making. Students work with repeatable simulated sensor data rather than ROS 2 so that they can focus on noise, bias, resolution, dropout, outliers, false detections, and limited range. The lab’s central idea is that a sensor does not reveal the world directly: it produces evidence with characteristic errors that a robot must characterize and manage.

In Mission 1, each student receives a reproducible assigned sensor condition and repeatedly samples it. They calculate and interpret mean, variance, bias, missing values, and outliers, visualize the distribution, and diagnose its dominant defect. Students must distinguish precision from accuracy and explain why a stable reading can still be wrong. The assignment is repeatable for grading while still requiring each student to analyze their own evidence.

Mission 2 introduces filtering and fusion through worked formulas and hand calculations. Students predict and record at least six configurations, including controlled comparisons of three moving-average windows, a matched moving-average/median pair, and three fusion weights. They inspect numerical outputs and measure noise reduction, error, availability, and sustained response delay. A heavily smoothed signal may look clean but react too slowly to a changing hazard; a median filter may reject isolated outliers while behaving differently under persistent bias. Students select a pipeline using quantitative criteria and explain why it is appropriate for the tested conditions rather than declaring one filter universally best.

Mission 3 turns estimates into decisions. Students predict, test, and revise separate policies for warehouse and assistive-robot scenarios. Each baseline and revision runs all seven noisy conditions. They compare false-safe rates, unnecessary-stop rates, detection delay, and dangerous-command events using fixed context benchmarks. Scenario replay and downloadable tables show how measurements lead to individual decisions. These are decision-risk proxies, not physical collision simulations. The acceptable trade-off may change with context: an unnecessary stop has a different consequence from moving toward a person or obstacle when evidence is unreliable. This makes uncertainty both a technical and sociotechnical design concern.

Students submit the assigned measurements, statistics, plots, diagnosis, complete filter experiment log, selected pipeline, time-series evidence, decision policies, scenario metrics, explanations, and final synthesis. The app generates an inspectable `student_submission/` directory and manifest. By the end, students should be able to characterize sensor error, choose and evaluate a filtering strategy, explain responsiveness-versus-smoothing trade-offs, and design a decision policy that explicitly accounts for uncertainty and the consequences of error.

The submission ends with a 150–250-word technical synthesis and a separate required individual reflection of 1–300 words. Students may use any of the course reflection prompts to connect the activity with their interests, motivation, human or societal considerations, and overall experience. Current-evidence checks, a hashed manifest, and a ZIP backup support reliable submission through a commit to the student's personal GitHub fork. Restorable autosaves and explicit navigation keep results visible and protect work across restarts.
